The Tria Prima

The concept of Tria Prima (or Three Principles) was introduced by Parlacesius, swiss alchemist, philosopher and phisitian of the XVI century. The Tria Prima are rappresented by sulfur, mercury and salt; and they form the base elements from which, following the alchemical tradition, all is formed. Alchemists believed that any material could be created by combining mercury and sulfur in different quantities. Later on, Parlacesius added salt as the third element to use during the alchemical process, which indicated materiality.

To better understand this concept, think of burning wood; the flames rappresent the combustability of sulfur, the smoke rappresents the volatility of mercury and the ashes rappresent the solidity of salt. It's important to keep in mind that these are not to be considered as elements themselves, but rather as mainly spiritual archetypes. In fact, following Parlacesius' vision, sulfur, mercury and slat corrispond respectively to soul, spirit and body, and through the alchemical process, they were to be freed from their physical aspect and transfigured in a spiritual sense, so to realize the Magnum Opus and reach internal transmutation.

The Classical Elements

In ancient Greek philosophy, the classification of the elements began with Empedocles in the 5th century BCE, who proposed that all matter is made of four basic “roots”: earth, water, air, and fire. These were seen as eternal substances that combine and separate to form everything in the natural world. Later, Aristotle refined this system by explaining the four elements in terms of qualities (hot, cold, wet, dry) and added a fifth element, aether (or quintessence), which he believed made up the unchanging heavens and celestial bodies.

The four elements were also linked to the four bodily “humors,” or fluids that were believed to determine health and temperament. Earth corresponded to black bile (associated with melancholy), water to phlegm (cold and wet, linked to calm or sluggishness), air to blood (warm and wet, linked to vitality and sociability), and fire to yellow bile (warm and dry, linked to anger or energy). Health was thought to depend on a balance of these humors, reflecting the same idea of harmony and mixture that Greek philosophers used to explain the natural world.

Metals and Processes

Regarding other imprtant things, alchemists also had extensive knowledge of metals and processes. Going into the specifics, there were the planetary metals and the alchemical processes: the first ones are a set of 7 metals, each connected to one of the planets in our system (at the time, Neptune and Uranus were not discovered, Earth was not considered a planet, differently from the Moon and the Sun who were in fact classified under this definition), the seconds were a set of 12 procedures that could be enacted on substances to alter them, each connected to one of the 12 zodiac signs.

There are of course other substances and more knowledge used by alchemists, but all can be considered in some way "secondary" or "marginal" next to what was shown to you up untill now.
